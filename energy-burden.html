Where energy bills
hurt most in Chicago.

Energy burden is the share of a household's income that goes to home energy. Across Chicago it averages about 2%, but that number hides a divided city. This map shows burden for every census tract, where it clusters, and what the spatial models say drives it.

What the models show

Four questions, four answers.

01

Where is burden concentrated?

In two large blocks. 175 tracts on the West Side and across the South Side form statistically significant high-burden clusters, while 203 tracts along the North lakefront form low-burden ones.

02

What explains it?

Income comes first, but not alone. In spatial Durbin models, chosen over simpler models by formal tests, tracts with more Black residents carry higher burden at the same income, counting spillover from neighboring tracts. Large apartment buildings lower burden, and older housing raises gas burden.

03

High bills or low incomes?

Both. Holding income and housing constant, a tract with 10 points more Black residents pays about 3.5% more for energy, and one with 10 points more Hispanic residents about 1.6% more. That bill gap holds in every robustness check.

04

Does it differ across the city?

Yes. Multiscale geographically weighted regression shows the income effect changes from one small group of about 18 tracts to the next, while renter share has no significant local effect anywhere. Explore it with the "Local effects" view on the map.

The core results (income, large apartment buildings, and higher bills in tracts with more Black residents) hold across alternative neighbor definitions, more reliable census estimates, newer 2020-2024 data, 200 simulations of census sampling error, estimators robust to unequal variance, and an independent burden measure built from census utility-cost tables (rank correlation 0.92). For policy, choosing tracts by median income reaches nearly as many severely burdened households as the best possible rule, and a 20% cost cut in the 175 priority tracts would lift about 16,000 households below the 6% line. These are neighborhood-level associations, not individual-level causes.

How it was built

Fully reproducible.

A Python pipeline downloads the public data, builds tract-level burden from 247,000 LEAD household cohorts, tests for clustering, fits OLS, spatial error, spatial lag, spatial Durbin, and MGWR models with formal model selection, runs robustness, measurement-error, and policy analyses, groups tracts into neighborhood types, and publishes every table, map, and notebook. Version 1.0.0 is frozen as a tagged release for citation. It reruns automatically on GitHub Actions whenever the code changes, and this map reads its data straight from that repository.
