Where energy bills
hurt most in Chicago.

Energy burden is the share of a household's income that goes to home energy. Across Chicago it averages about 2%, but that number hides a divided city. This map shows burden for every census tract, where it clusters, and what the spatial models say drives it.

What the models show

Four questions, four answers.

01

Where is burden concentrated?

In two large blocks. 175 tracts on the West Side and across the South Side form statistically significant high-burden clusters, while 203 tracts along the North lakefront form low-burden ones.

02

What explains it?

Income comes first, but not alone. In spatial error models, tracts with more Black and Hispanic residents carry higher burden at the same income. Older housing raises burden; large apartment buildings lower it.

03

High bills or low incomes?

Both. Holding income and housing constant, a tract with 10 points more Black residents pays about 2.8% more for energy, so much of the racial gap in burden comes from higher bills.

04

Does it differ across the city?

Yes. Multiscale geographically weighted regression shows the income effect changes from one small group of tracts to the next, while renter share has the same weak effect citywide.

Results hold across alternative neighbor definitions, more reliable census estimates, newer 2020-2024 data, and an independent burden measure built from census utility-cost tables (rank correlation 0.92). These are neighborhood-level associations, not individual-level causes.

How it was built

Fully reproducible.

A Python pipeline downloads the public data, builds tract-level burden from 247,000 LEAD household cohorts, tests for clustering, fits OLS, spatial error, spatial Durbin, and MGWR models, runs five robustness checks, and publishes every table and map. It reruns automatically on GitHub Actions whenever the code changes, and this map reads its data straight from that repository.
